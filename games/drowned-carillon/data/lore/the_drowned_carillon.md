# The Drowned Carillon

## Nature of the Threat
The Carillon is not a god and there is nothing to banish. It is a cathedral organ under the sea off the Brass Coast, intact, and the tide plays it. What it does is not flooding. It tunes. It takes a living thing and leaves salt, verdigris, and a chime where a heartbeat used to be, and the thing goes on standing where it stood.

## It Answers Bells
Nothing has spoken. What it does is respond. Sound anything on this coast with a note in it and something under the water sounds back, and it holds the interval afterwards the way a man holds a promise. The shore-bell on Bellfounders' Quay was rung once, in the year of the three tides. The Carillon has been holding the second half of that phrase ever since, and every year it is a little louder.

## It Feeds on Song and Grief
It does not take the strong or the careless, which is the fact that took the coast longest to admit. It takes the mourned. The more cleanly a thing has been sung over, the more cleanly it can be sounded out. That is why the boats stopped singing, why nobody names a child after a drowned relative any more, and why Salt-Widow Vesh, who has lost three, hears it more clearly than anyone alive.

## Signs at the Water Line
Brass off the flats that will not warm in the hand. Set-nets that come up sorted by pitch. A gull that rings. A tide that runs a few minutes early and then a few more. Anyone can keep the table. Almost nobody compares theirs to the one on the notice-board post, because comparing is the point at which it stops being the sea and becomes an appointment.

## Why Nobody Stops It
Stilling a thing requires naming it, and naming is the costly half. There is no fleet to meet and no gate to hold; there is a stair, and it is under water for twenty hours of every day. The only measurable check on the Carillon in eleven years has been an old man declining to pull a rope.
