# The Lantern Watch

## Four People and a Great Many Lamps
The Watch of Tallowmere is called the Lantern Watch, and its officers are called Lanterns, after the poles they carry on their beats. There are four sworn Lanterns, a sergeant and a captain, and between them they answer for every street in the city from the Lantern House, where the sergeants' desk, the charge-book and the cells are. The Watch also keeps the lamp ordinance: the lamplighters' round is licensed by the Lantern House, and a lamp left dark is reported there.

## Captain Ardane
Sharp, fair, tireless and certain. Captain Ardane has hunted the Magpie for two years and knows every line of the list of what was taken. She is honest, which is exactly what makes her the most dangerous person in the city to be mistaken for a thief by: she cannot be bought, she does not forget, and she is usually right.

## Sergeant Brask
Large, slow, fond of pastry and of money he did not have to earn. Brask keeps the desk and the charge-book at the Lantern House, and almost everything about him has a price, including looking the other way. A file that goes missing from Brask's desk stays missing.

## The Lanterns on the Beat
Lantern Tully is young, earnest and in his first month on the docks, and he is the one who shouted "the Magpie!" on the quay. Lantern Hobb has walked nights for twenty years and is in no hurry about any of it. A Lantern on his beat is a man with sore feet, a drawing of the Magpie folded in his coat, and a lamp he swings into faces out of habit.

## How the Watch Hears Things
The Watch does not see everything. It hears things: from a stallholder who counts your change twice, from a householder who shouts for help, from a porter in a taproom, from the lamplighter's round. Word moves through Tallowmere person to person and reaches the Lantern House a little blurred. The Watch divides the city three ways -- the quay, Wickmarket and the Rise -- and a watch-house that has heard of you is not the same as one that has not.

## The Cells and the Green
The cells under the Lantern House are cold, and the charge-book decides how long a guest stays in them or what they pay to leave. The Watch hangs thieves on Gallows Green, and the Green is not a metaphor.

## Going Out Without Leave
There is a third way out of the cells, and every guest the Lantern House has ever kept has looked at it: the Lantern on the cells door sleeps in his chair with the keys on a nail, and one bar in the window has been rusting in its socket since before the captain was born. A guest who is caught at it gets a hiding and goes back behind the door. A guest who is not caught walks out still owing everything the charge-book says, and the Lantern House writes the empty cell in the book as a charge of its own. After the first time, it chains the keys to a Lantern's belt; it has never got round to the window.
