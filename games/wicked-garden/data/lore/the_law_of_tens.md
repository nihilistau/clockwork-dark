# The Law Of Tens

## One For Ten
One hour in the Garden is roughly ten hours in the waking world. One day is ten days. A season here, measured by nothing more astronomical than the mood of the court, is frequently years outside. This is the first law a guest is told and it is told early and plainly, because the Sovereign does not consider it a trap. She considers it a price, and she is genuinely offended by the suggestion that quoting a price is the same as hiding one.

Stay a week and someone may bury an empty casket with your name cut into it. Stay a season and your name is a story told wrong by people who are not sure they ever met you.

## The Moth With The Calendar
On the Path of First Petals a moth of pale glass will land on a new guest's sleeve, once, and open its wings, and what is on them is a calendar coming apart in tens — days peeling off in handfuls. It is not a warning. It is a receipt being issued in advance. The moth does this for everyone. Nobody has ever established whether it is doing it on instruction.

## The Toll Is Charged At Night
The debt is not paid when you leave. It accrues nightly, at the close of each Garden day, whether the day was spent in bed or in the labyrinth or arguing with a courtier about the correct way to hold a glass. There is no efficient use of a day here. There is only a day, and what it cost, and the number is the same either way. This is the single most important thing a guest can understand and the one they understand last, because for the first three days the number is small enough to be interesting.

## What The Debt Is Actually Spent On
The days do not go anywhere. That is the part that catches people. The waking world is not paused and it is not waiting; it is simply continuing without the debtor in it, at ten times the rate they are living, and everything that happens in it happens whether they are there or not. Illnesses run their course. Leases lapse. People decide, quite reasonably, what the disappearance meant. A guest who spends nine Garden days on a love affair has spent three mortal months on it and has been declared missing twice.

## The Three Doors
There are three ways out of the Garden and every one of them is a law rather than a route. Her leave, freely given, which is the cleanest and the rarest. An older law correctly invoked, which requires knowing the words and getting them right on the first attempt. And death, which works. Mortals invent a fourth door and call it cleverness. The labyrinth's statue clearings are largely populated by the ones who did.

## What Leaving Whole Costs Her

Letting a mortal go home intact is not merely a concession of pride. The Garden's ledger does not simply forget a claim that was cultivated and then released; something has to be paid down, and the payment comes out of the Sovereign's own standing with the root beneath her. She has never itemised this to anyone. What is observable from outside is that the Sovereigns who let people go whole have short reigns, and that she knows this, and that it is one of perhaps three facts about her position she has never once made into a joke.
