# The Unnamed Saints

## A Faith Without Names
The shrine at Edgewood is dedicated to saints nobody can name. This is not forgetting. The practice is deliberate and it is old: the saints are not named because naming is how a thing is reached, and a thing that can be reached can be reached from the other side too. What is left is a wall, a candle, and a habit.

## The Two Candles
The old women light two candles now where they used to light one. Asked why, they say the second is for the saint whose turn it is, which explains nothing and is clearly a formula. The change began the same month the well went to iron. Nobody in the village has connected the two out loud.

## Offices and Observances
There are no priests. There are four observances a year, run by whoever is oldest and willing, and a set of responses that are sung rather than spoken because the words are worn past sense. The responses are in three verses. This matters, later, to somebody.

## What the Saints Were For
The stories that survive are all the same shape: a person stood between a village and something, gave up their name to do it, and the village survived and could not thank them by name afterwards. That is the whole theology. It is not comforting and was never meant to be. It is instructions.

## The Order of Saint Nine
There is a fragmentary tradition, mostly in the Marches, that there were nine of them, and that the ninth is the one who is still standing. Tinkers sell ward pins nine to a card and will tell you it is coincidence. Tinkers say a great many things are coincidence.

## Why It Is Not Superstition
Everything in the unnamed practice — do not name it, do not write it down, keep the ordinary hours — is precisely what a village would do if it had once learned, expensively, that attention is what the thing travels along. The faith is a data-loss protocol dressed as devotion, and it has outlived every kingdom that laughed at it.
