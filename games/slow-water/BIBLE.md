# The Slow Water — story bible

Nine days upriver on a funeral barge. You are a hired mourner: paid by the
day to weep for strangers so that a family need not weep in public. The
Ravel family is carrying their father upriver to be buried at the source,
because the old rite says a man goes back the way the water came. You have
done this eleven times. You are good at it.

By the second day you know the family is not grieving. By the fourth you
know why. The box in the hold does not hold the man whose name is on it,
and every person aboard is waiting to see whether the professional weeper
is the sort who counts, or the sort who is paid not to.

The story is the nine days. Nothing is chased and nothing is fought. What
moves is what the family will say in front of you, and what you can afford
to let your face do.

## Places (4)

**The afterdeck** (entry) — where a mourner is meant to stand and be seen
working. Rope, wet boards, the wake unrolling behind. The only place aboard
you are alone and still visible.

**The saloon** — the long cabin where the Ravels eat three times a day
whether or not they have anything to say. Brass lamps, a table too fine for
a boat, nine chairs and eight of them used.

**The hold** — low, cold, the box on trestles with river light coming
through the boards in bars. The lid is screwed, not nailed. You have
noticed the screws are new.

**The towpath** — where everyone walks when the river runs too shallow to
push. Mud, horses, a mile of forced company. Things get said on the towpath
that are not said aboard.

## People

**Iva Ravel**, the eldest daughter (an AGENT — she gets her own voice, her
own prompt file). She hired you. She watches you work the way a butcher
watches another butcher. She is the only one aboard who is not lying to
you, which does not mean she is telling you the truth.

**Tobin Ravel**, the youngest son — twenty, drinks, wants very badly for
someone to ask him a direct question.

**Sabra Corriveau**, the boatmaster — not family, paid like you, been up
this river forty times and has opinions about the ones that came back
light.

**Father Elm**, the river priest — travelling to perform the rite, doing
the arithmetic of what he is willing not to notice.

## Things

A **day-book** where you record what you were paid for and what you actually
did, which are not the same column.

A set of **borrowed blacks** — mourning clothes that belonged to someone
else's funeral, taken in at the seams.

A **screwdriver** you did not bring aboard and did not steal, which was in
your coat on the fourth morning.

## Meters

**Favor** is the family's regard — whether the Ravels want you at the
table. **Composure** is the mask: the professional grief, the face that
does not cost anything. Spending composure is how you feel something real
in front of people who will notice. The **suspicion** clock is how close
the family has come to knowing that you have worked it out.

The interesting beats move favor and composure in opposite directions.
Being liked and being honest are not the same act aboard this boat.

## Tone

Warm grief, river light, quiet menace. This is a story about work — about
being paid to perform an emotion and what that does to the emotion. Plain
language, concrete nouns, weather and food and rope. No supernatural
element: the river is only a river and the dead man is only dead.

Nothing explicit; the ceiling is suggestive. The distress here is
bereavement, class, and complicity, handled with warmth rather than shock.
