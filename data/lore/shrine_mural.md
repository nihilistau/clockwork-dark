# The Shrine Mural

## An Unfinished Wall
The shrine's lime-washed wall bears a mural: saints with missing faces, wheat threaded with brass, a road winding inward. It was never finished. Old women say finishing it would invite the road to arrive, which everyone treats as a charming local superstition and which is a fairly precise account of the situation.

## The Faces Were Not Lost
The standard explanation is that the plaster failed and the faces flaked. The plaster did not fail; it is sound and original across the whole upper register. The faces were never painted. Somebody left them, deliberately, on a wall where every other detail down to the wheat awns is finished to a hair.

## What the Last Panel Shows
Bottom right, and the last thing put on the wall: a road coming in. Not leaving — the perspective is unambiguous and it is the only element in the composition that moves toward the viewer. It stops mid-stroke. The brush was set down and the pigment on the tray dried in the tray, and it is still there under sixty years of soot.

## The Painter
She was not from Edgewood and nobody recorded her name, which given the dedication of the shrine may have been the point. She worked eleven months. She is under the barrow — the ninth one — and the village that buried her there has never explained why a painter got a barrow burial when the barrows are older than the village and nobody else has ever been put in one.

## Reading It
A traveller with lore enough can read the register properly and it is not devotional art, it is a sequence. Nine panels, left to right, and the ninth is the unfinished one. Read as a sequence, the earlier panels are not saints being venerated. They are a method being documented, step by step, by someone who expected to be dead before it was needed.

## Finishing It
There is a strong local prohibition against touching the wall, and there is a small, quiet, entirely separate tradition — two families, no more — that the wall is supposed to be finished eventually, by somebody, and that the prohibition exists to make sure it is not finished by the wrong somebody at the wrong time. Both traditions agree the pigment recipe is written on the back of the tray.
